I started on a basketball court. Not a research lab, not a coding bootcamp, not a university lecture hall. A basketball court. I learned something there that I still use every day: **discipline is not about talent. It is about showing up when the result is uncertain.** You do not know if the shot will go in. You practice anyway. That mindset — showing up, iterating, trusting the process — is the only reason I am a researcher today.

After basketball, I found myself in a classroom. But not as a student. As a teacher. For over **ten years**, I have stood in front of students explaining economics, mathematics, and eventually programming. And for several of those years, I have been teaching **French** — a language I learned formally through certification at the Institut français and refined through advanced study at the Open University UK. Teaching French taught me something coding never could: **how to make complex systems feel intuitive to someone who has never encountered them before.** Every time I explain a neural network to a beginner, I am using the same skill I use to explain French verb conjugations: breaking the complex into the inevitable.

I am still teaching French today — it is my current primary teaching role alongside economics and programming. I do not see it as a side job. I see it as part of the same mission: **making knowledge accessible to people the system assumes cannot access it.** The student who struggles with Python syntax and the student who struggles with French subjunctive are facing the same barrier — not inability, but exposure. My job is to engineer that exposure.

## The Pivot: From Chalkboard to Code

My formal training was in economics — a Bachelor of Science that gave me frameworks but not tools. I could describe market failures. I could not model them. I could explain inflation. I could not visualize its trajectory across a decade. That gap between understanding and doing is what drove me to Python, to data science, and eventually to AI research.

The turning point was a single project. In 2023, I analyzed national inflation data using Python for the first time. Within hours, I built visualizations that would have taken weeks with traditional methods. I saw policy decisions from 2016 still rippling through the economy in 2023. And I realized: **my students needed these tools, not just the theory I was explaining.**

That project became the Inflation Dashboard analyzing national economic data. It also became the seed of a larger question that now drives my research: *If AI and automation are reshaping global labor markets, what happens to young people entering those markets with limited digital skills?* This is not abstract. I have taught students who share one smartphone. I have mentored graduates who lose remote job opportunities because their internet cannot sustain a video call. The digital divide is not a concept. It is a daily reality I witness in classrooms.

> **The Moment Everything Connected**
>
> I was teaching Python to a group of students who shared one laptop among five. The lesson was on data visualization. One student, who had never touched a computer before the course, created a bar chart showing unemployment rates by sector. She looked at the screen and said: "Now I can see what I was only guessing before."
>
> That sentence — *"Now I can see"* — is why I research. Data literacy is not a career skill. It is a form of sight.

## What I Built: Credentials That Bridge Theory and Practice

Teaching showed me the problems. Formal training gave me the methods to solve them. Over the past two years, I pursued four credentials that transformed what I could offer students, collaborators, and the research community:

- **Foundations of Responsible AI** at Northeastern University — Ethics as design principle, not afterthought. Essential when your research affects vulnerable populations.
- **Data Science Bootcamp** at AI Planet — Practical Python, machine learning, and visualization. Skills I now teach and expand in my own courses.
- **Associate Degree in Computer Science** at University of the People (in progress) — Formal CS foundations to complement economics background. Expected completion 2026–2027.
- **FLE & ESL Teaching Certificates** from Institut français and British Council — Pedagogical training that shapes how I design courses for diverse learners.

Each credential was earned while teaching full-time — French in the mornings, economics and programming in the afternoons, my own coursework at night. The discipline I learned on the basketball court is the only reason this schedule is possible. You do not manage that load through motivation. You manage it through routine.

## The Doors That Opened — And the One That Did Not

In 2025, I did something that felt impossible: I applied to graduate programs in the United States. My background was unconventional — economics, teaching, self-taught programming, online certifications, a decade in classrooms rather than labs. I prepared my statements, gathered recommendations, and submitted applications without knowing if admissions committees would see value in a path that did not fit their typical template.

They did. The responses came back, and they were not rejections. They were invitations — with funding, with recognition, with validation that a decade of teaching and self-directed learning could compete with traditional pipelines.

## Why I Still Teach French While Studying AI

Some people ask why I still teach French when I am pursuing graduate work in data science and AI. The answer is simple: **language and code are the same skill viewed from different angles.** Both require pattern recognition. Both demand patience with ambiguity. Both are tools for accessing systems that would otherwise exclude you.

When I teach a student their first French sentence, I am teaching them that complex systems can be learned. When I teach a student their first Python function, I am teaching them the exact same thing. The medium changes. The lesson does not.

I do not know where this path ends. I know only that it started on a basketball court, moved through a language classroom, and is now heading into a research lab. The through-line is not a field. It is a habit: **show up, iterate, trust the process.** The shot may not go in. You practice anyway.
