From the Basketball Court to the Lab: A Researcher Built on Discipline

I learned teamwork on a basketball court, patience in a language classroom, and persistence in the face of closed doors. Admitted to six graduate programs — including Northeastern, Boston University, and the University of Pittsburgh — this is how those three things became a research career, and why I still teach French while studying AI.

I started on a basketball court. Not a research lab, not a coding bootcamp, not a university lecture hall. A basketball court. I learned something there that I still use every day: discipline is not about talent. It is about showing up when the result is uncertain. You do not know if the shot will go in. You practice anyway. That mindset — showing up, iterating, trusting the process — is the only reason I am a researcher today.

After basketball, I found myself in a classroom. But not as a student. As a teacher. For over ten years, I have stood in front of students explaining economics, mathematics, and eventually programming. And for several of those years, I have been teaching French — a language I learned formally through certification at the Institut français and refined through advanced study at the Open University UK. Teaching French taught me something coding never could: how to make complex systems feel intuitive to someone who has never encountered them before. Every time I explain a neural network to a beginner, I am using the same skill I use to explain French verb conjugations: breaking the complex into the inevitable.

I am still teaching French today — it is my current primary teaching role alongside economics and programming. I do not see it as a side job. I see it as part of the same mission: making knowledge accessible to people the system assumes cannot access it. The student who struggles with Python syntax and the student who struggles with French subjunctive are facing the same barrier — not inability, but exposure. My job is to engineer that exposure.

The Pivot: From Chalkboard to Code

My formal training was in economics — a Bachelor of Science that gave me frameworks but not tools. I could describe market failures. I could not model them. I could explain inflation. I could not visualize its trajectory across a decade. That gap between understanding and doing is what drove me to Python, to data science, and eventually to AI research.

The turning point was a single project. In 2023, I analyzed national inflation data using Python for the first time. Within hours, I built visualizations that would have taken weeks with traditional methods. I saw policy decisions from 2016 still rippling through the economy in 2023. And I realized: my students needed these tools, not just the theory I was explaining.

That project became the Inflation Dashboard analyzing national economic data. It also became the seed of a larger question that now drives my research: If AI and automation are reshaping global labor markets, what happens to young people entering those markets with limited digital skills? This is not abstract. I have taught students who share one smartphone. I have mentored graduates who lose remote job opportunities because their internet cannot sustain a video call. The digital divide is not a concept. It is a daily reality I witness in classrooms.

The Moment Everything Connected

I was teaching Python to a group of students who shared one laptop among five. The lesson was on data visualization. One student, who had never touched a computer before the course, created a bar chart showing unemployment rates by sector. She looked at the screen and said: "Now I can see what I was only guessing before."

That sentence — "Now I can see" — is why I research. Data literacy is not a career skill. It is a form of sight.

What I Built: Credentials That Bridge Theory and Practice

Teaching showed me the problems. Formal training gave me the methods to solve them. Over the past two years, I pursued four credentials that transformed what I could offer students, collaborators, and the research community:

  • Foundations of Responsible AI at Northeastern University — Ethics as design principle, not afterthought. Essential when your research affects vulnerable populations.
  • Data Science Bootcamp at AI Planet — Practical Python, machine learning, and visualization. Skills I now teach and expand in my own courses.
  • Associate Degree in Computer Science at University of the People (in progress) — Formal CS foundations to complement economics background. Expected completion 2026–2027.
  • FLE & ESL Teaching Certificates from Institut français and British Council — Pedagogical training that shapes how I design courses for diverse learners.

Each credential was earned while teaching full-time — French in the mornings, economics and programming in the afternoons, my own coursework at night. The discipline I learned on the basketball court is the only reason this schedule is possible. You do not manage that load through motivation. You manage it through routine.

The Doors That Opened — And the One That Did Not

In 2025, I did something that felt impossible: I applied to graduate programs in the United States. My background was unconventional — economics, teaching, self-taught programming, online certifications, a decade in classrooms rather than labs. I prepared my statements, gathered recommendations, and submitted applications without knowing if admissions committees would see value in a path that did not fit their typical template.

They did. The responses came back, and they were not rejections. They were invitations — with funding, with recognition, with validation that a decade of teaching and self-directed learning could compete with traditional pipelines.

I could not attend. Travel documentation constraints — a bureaucratic wall I could not code around, could not teach through, could not visualize away. I watched peers with fewer credentials board planes while I stayed behind. That is a specific kind of frustration: knowing you are capable, knowing you are admitted, knowing you have partial funding, and still being stopped by a stamp you cannot control.

But here is what basketball taught me: you do not stop playing because the shot was blocked. You adjust your position and take the next one.

I applied again for Fall 2026. I refined my statements, strengthened my research profile, and submitted to programs that valued interdisciplinary backgrounds. And the doors opened wider:

Graduate Admissions (Fall 2026)

  • University of Pittsburgh — PhD in Information Science
  • Catholic University of America — MS in Data Analytics

I am still waiting. The documentation issue is not resolved. But I am not waiting passively. I am teaching French. I am completing my UoPeople degree. I am submitting my OMEE paper. I am building this platform. I am doing what I have always done: showing up and working while the outcome is uncertain.

What I Research: The Question From the Classroom

My current paper, "Artificial Intelligence, Youth Employment, and the Digital Divide: A Practitioner-Informed Analysis," submitted to the OMEE journal, is the direct result of this trajectory. The term "practitioner-informed" is deliberate — it means the research is grounded in classrooms and job markets I have personally witnessed, not abstract theory I read in a library.

The paper asks: As AI automates jobs globally, what happens to young people entering the labor market with limited digital skills? I have taught these students. I have watched them graduate into economies that demand skills their schools never taught. I have mentored them through scholarship applications they should not have to navigate alone. This research is not about them. It is for them.

My argument is simple and hard: AI policy for developing economies cannot import frameworks from technology hubs. It must account for infrastructure gaps, educational disparities, and the reality that the world's youngest population will be concentrated in regions with the least digital access. We are not preparing for a distant future. We are living it now.

What I Can Do: Capabilities Built on Three Fields

I do not fit neatly into one category. I am not just an economist, not just a programmer, not just a teacher, not just a researcher. I am someone who moves between those roles because the problems I care about require all of them. Here is what I bring:

  • Research design & analysis: Econometric frameworks, data visualization, Python-based statistical analysis, and policy-relevant interpretation for resource-constrained contexts
  • Teaching & curriculum development: 10+ years designing courses for low-connectivity environments, from French language to Python programming to research methods
  • Cross-cultural collaboration: Advanced French (FLE-certified) and English (ESL-certified), enabling work across diverse research and educational ecosystems
  • Ethical AI application: Northeastern certification in responsible AI, ensuring research and tools are designed with equity as core principle
  • Scholarship navigation: Deep research into international education pathways and the specific challenges underrepresented applicants face — informed by personal experience

What I Believe: The Mission

I believe the distance between where a student starts and where they can go is measured not in geography or income, but in access to knowledge and tools. I believe a student with limited connectivity can access the same AI education as a student in a well-equipped lab — if the education is designed for their reality. I believe a researcher from any background can contribute to global conversations on AI and employment, if the systems are built to include them.

I believe in showing up. I learned that on a basketball court, refined it in classrooms teaching French and economics, and apply it every day in my research and my own education. The shot may not go in. The documentation may not arrive. The paper may be rejected. But the discipline of preparation is never wasted. It compounds. It builds capability that outlasts any single outcome.

"I am a basketball player who became a French teacher, who became an economics instructor, who became a Python programmer, who became an AI researcher. The thread is not talent. The thread is discipline. The thread is showing up. The thread is refusing to let a closed door be the end of the story."

What Comes Next

My immediate priorities are completing my Associate Degree in Computer Science at UoPeople, securing publication of my OMEE paper, and resolving the documentation constraints that stand between me and my PhD at the University of Pittsburgh. Beyond that, I am actively pursuing MS/PhD opportunities that value interdisciplinary research with demonstrable social impact.

But my larger vision is unchanged: a world where students are not passive recipients of technological change, but active architects of it. Where a learner with limited resources can access the same analytical tools as anyone. Where a researcher from any background can contribute to global conversations on AI and employment. Where a closed door is a detour, not a destination.

That vision requires collaborators, mentors, and partners. If you are building something aligned with it — in research, education, or policy — I want to hear from you. I am still teaching French. I am still coding at night. I am still showing up. And I am still taking the next shot.
